Bitcoin Correlation with the S&P 500 and US Dollar: A Rolling Analysis Guide

Bitcoin is sometimes discussed as if it always behaves like a technology stock, a hedge, or a direct response to the US dollar. None of those descriptions is reliable in every period. A rolling correlation is a way to measure how two return series moved together over a selected window—not a rule explaining why they moved or what happens next.

What correlation measures

Correlation summarizes the direction and strength of a linear relationship between two series. It ranges from -1 to +1: values near +1 indicate that returns tended to move in the same direction over the sample; values near -1 indicate movement in opposite directions; values around zero indicate little linear co-movement in that window. Correlation does not measure causation, risk by itself or the size of an asset's moves.

For market comparisons, use returns rather than comparing raw index levels. A common choice is daily percentage returns, although log returns may be used for some analyses. Match timestamps and trading calendars carefully: Bitcoin trades continuously, while US equity markets have sessions, holidays and gaps.

Why use a rolling window?

A single correlation calculated over several years can conceal changes between market regimes. A rolling 30-, 60- or 90-observation window recalculates the relationship as new observations arrive. Shorter windows react faster but are noisier; longer windows are smoother but can lag a regime shift. The window is a research choice, not a universally correct setting.

Illustrative workflow
  1. Choose a consistent daily return series for Bitcoin and the S&P 500.
  2. Choose a clearly defined dollar measure, such as a broad US dollar index, and document its source.
  3. Align observations by date and decide how to handle weekends and market holidays.
  4. Calculate rolling correlation for a stated window, such as 60 matched observations.
  5. Plot the result beside both assets' returns and volatility.
  6. Repeat with a different window to see whether the conclusion depends on the parameter.

Interpret the S&P 500 comparison carefully

A positive rolling correlation means Bitcoin and the equity index had a tendency to move in the same direction during that sample. It does not prove Bitcoin is an equity substitute. The relationship may reflect shared sensitivity to liquidity, risk appetite or a major news event; it can also weaken or reverse. Compare multiple windows and inspect large outlier days rather than treating one correlation reading as a permanent characteristic.

Interpret the US dollar comparison carefully

A dollar index is not the same thing as US interest rates, global liquidity or every investor's funding currency. A negative correlation in one window means the two return series tended to move in opposite directions in that sample. It does not establish that dollar strength caused a Bitcoin decline. The result may change with the chosen dollar measure, dates, return frequency and market regime.

Common analytical traps

  • Level correlation: trending price levels can produce misleading relationships; compare returns for most co-movement questions.
  • Calendar mismatch: Bitcoin weekend observations and equity-market closures can distort alignment.
  • Window shopping: testing many windows and reporting only the most dramatic result overstates confidence.
  • Outlier dependence: a small number of extreme days can dominate a short sample.
  • Correlation equals causation: co-movement alone cannot identify the driver.
  • Look-ahead bias: a historical chart must use only information available at each point if it is used to evaluate a decision process.

A compact research note template

Record the data provider, ticker or index definition, time zone, sampling frequency, return method, observation window, missing-data treatment, calculation date and any major outliers. State the result as a description of that sample. Include a caveat that relationships can change and that correlation is not a price forecast.
