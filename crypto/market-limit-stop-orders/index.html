Market, limit and stop orders: what is the difference?

An order tells a trading platform how you want a buy or sell instruction handled. The important distinction is that some orders prioritize execution, while others set a price condition—and none removes market risk.

Market order: prioritize execution

A market order asks the platform to buy or sell as soon as possible at the best prices currently available. It generally prioritizes getting the trade executed, not guaranteeing the exact price. In a fast-moving or thin market, the final execution price can differ from the last price you saw.

Limit order: set a price boundary

A limit order specifies the highest price you are willing to pay when buying, or the lowest price you are willing to accept when selling. It can help you avoid accepting a worse price than your limit, but it may never execute if the market does not reach your price—or if there is not enough available liquidity.

Stop order: activate after a trigger

A stop order becomes active when a specified stop price is reached. In the common stop-market structure described by FINRA for securities, reaching the stop price turns the instruction into a market order. That means the execution price is not guaranteed and can differ from the stop price. Some platforms offer other stop-order variants, so read the platform's exact rules.

Compare the trade-offs

  • Market: prioritizes execution; the price can move before the order fills.
  • Limit: sets a maximum buy price or minimum sell price; execution is not guaranteed.
  • Stop-market: waits for a trigger, then seeks market execution; the final price is not guaranteed.

Questions to check on a crypto platform

  • Does the platform support the order type for the specific asset and trading pair?
  • Does a stop trigger create a market order or a limit order?
  • How does the platform define its trigger price—last trade, mark price, index price, or another reference?
  • What fees, minimum sizes, liquidity conditions, and trading interruptions apply?
  • Can an order be partially filled, rejected, or cancelled under the platform's rules?

Order names can look familiar across platforms, but their detailed mechanics may differ. Read the exchange or broker's own documentation before placing an order. This explainer uses FINRA's general securities-market descriptions as a starting point, not as a claim that every crypto venue follows identical rules.
