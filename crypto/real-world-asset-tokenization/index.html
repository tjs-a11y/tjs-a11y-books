What is real-world asset tokenization?

"Tokenizing" a building or a bond means putting a claim on it onto a blockchain. Here is how that is done, what it is supposed to improve, and where the real risk sits.

The one-sentence version

Real-world asset (RWA) tokenization turns ownership of an off-chain asset — real estate, Treasuries, commodities, art — into tokens recorded on a blockchain, so the claim can be held, split and transferred digitally.

How it works, step by step

  1. Wrap the asset legally. The real asset is placed into a legal structure — a special-purpose vehicle (SPV), a trust, or a regulated custodian — so the token represents an enforceable claim on something real.
  2. Verify and custody it. A licensed custodian typically confirms the asset exists and has not been pledged twice.
  3. Mint the tokens. Tokens are issued on a blockchain (often Ethereum or Polygon), frequently so the asset can be owned in fractions.
  4. Distribute and service. Fractions are sold to investors, who may receive income the asset produces — for example, rent from a tokenized property.

What it is supposed to improve

  • Fractional access. People can buy a small slice of an expensive asset instead of the whole thing.
  • Always-on settlement. Public blockchains run 24/7, unlike traditional market hours.
  • Fewer middlemen, in theory. Proponents argue removing intermediaries can cut cost and settlement time.
  • Auditability. A public ledger can make ownership and transfers easier to trace.

These are the benefits the industry claims; whether a given project delivers them depends entirely on how it is built and regulated.

Where the risk actually sits

  • The token is only as good as the legal claim behind it. Most of the risk is off-chain — how the asset is held, priced and enforced — not in the blockchain itself.
  • Regulatory uncertainty. Legal treatment is still unsettled, and it gets murkier across borders. Projects often require identity checks, investor eligibility and transfer restrictions.
  • Technical limits. Moving assets across different blockchains raises composability and scalability problems.
  • Volatility and liquidity. A token being tradable does not guarantee a buyer at a fair price.

Industry estimates of the market's size vary widely and are not settled facts — figures around $20 billion+ today and multi-trillion-dollar forecasts for 2030 come from industry players, so treat them as (estimate), not established numbers.
